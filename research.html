How do bacteria coordinate the work of staying alive?

I am a microbiologist interested in how bacteria organize growth, build their cell envelopes, and adapt their physiology to changing conditions. My doctoral work focused on the molecular regulation of cell-wall synthesis in Mycobacterium smegmatis. Across projects, I combine bacterial genetics, protein biochemistry, microscopy, and quantitative analysis to test how molecular mechanisms shape cellular behavior.

From individual proteins to coordinated cell biology

A bacterial cell is not a collection of independent pathways. It must coordinate envelope construction, growth, division, and responses to stress while keeping its physical barriers intact. In my doctoral research, I studied how regulatory proteins and metabolic intermediates help connect these processes in mycobacteria.

That work shaped the questions I bring to research: What is the mechanism? Where and when does it operate in the cell? What evidence would distinguish a direct effect from an indirect one? And how far can we generalize from a molecular result to a physiological outcome?

My research experience has moved between genetics, biochemical mechanism, protein interaction, and cell-level imaging. Each approach provides a different kind of evidence; the strongest explanations emerge when those lines of evidence are tested against one another.

Research program · Doctoral work

Coordinating the mycobacterial cell envelope

My PhD research asked how bacteria coordinate two major layers of the cell envelope even though those layers are assembled through distinct biosynthetic pathways.

01

PgfA: linking peptidoglycan and mycolate synthesis

The question. How does a growing mycobacterial cell coordinate its peptidoglycan (PG) layer with its lipid-rich outer membrane, or mycomembrane?

The approach. I combined targeted mutagenesis and complementation with chemical perturbations, fluorescent metabolic probes, and quantitative fluorescence microscopy. HADA/NADA labeling helped assess PG synthesis, while DMN-trehalose provided a readout related to mycolic-acid biosynthesis.

What the work showed. My first-author study identified a regulatory role for the polar growth factor PgfA in coordinating polar PG synthesis with mycolate synthesis. The results support a model in which availability of the shared metabolic intermediate trehalose monomycolate (TMM) helps connect envelope-building pathways. I also characterized regulatory threonine phosphorylation sites in PgfA’s intrinsically disordered region and investigated their relationship to cell-wall organization and antibiotic susceptibility.

Why it matters. The work provides a mechanistic example of how a bacterium can coordinate distinct envelope layers rather than regulate each pathway in isolation.

02

MurJ: understanding the regulation of an essential flippase

The question. How is MurJ, the essential lipid II flippase that moves a peptidoglycan precursor across the membrane, regulated in mycobacteria?

The approach. I used CRISPR interference, domain-deletion genetics, and complementary experiments to examine MurJ’s regulatory architecture and test a proposed interaction between MurJ and the regulatory protein CwlM. The interaction model was evaluated using AlphaFold2 Multimer, a mycobacterial two-hybrid assay, and co-immunoprecipitation.

What the work showed. The work supported opposing regulatory roles for MurJ domains: the periplasmic substrate-binding domain promotes activity, while the cytoplasmic domain can inhibit it. It also showed that the regulatory architecture is not functionally interchangeable between M. smegmatis and M. tuberculosis. Across three complementary approaches, we did not validate the previously proposed MurJ–CwlM interaction.

Why it matters. This project reinforced the importance of testing attractive mechanistic models with independent evidence—and of distinguishing a result in a model organism from a conclusion about a related pathogen.

What’s next

From envelope coordination to single-cell state.

At UiT, I’m turning the same mechanistic habits toward a different question: why do genetically identical bacteria differ in what they are doing?

The question

Within a clonal Staphylococcus aureus population, individual cells differ in the activity of their serine hydrolases. Activity-based probes report the active-enzyme state of a cell, not enzyme abundance and not enzyme identity. I want to know what sets that state, and when it matters.

The approach

I’m bringing quantitative single-cell imaging together with proteome-level measures of enzyme conformation and activity, and transcriptional profiling of bacteria in host-like contexts. The aim is to separate regulation of activity from differences in expression, and to describe how heterogeneity co-occurs with host conditions without over-claiming causation.

The specific project is being shaped with Dr. Christian Lentz and is subject to change as data come in.
