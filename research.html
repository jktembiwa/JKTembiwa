Research · Mechanism, context, and cellular behavior

How do bacteria coordinate the work of staying alive?

I am a microbiologist interested in how bacteria organize growth, build their cell envelopes, and adapt their physiology to changing conditions. My doctoral work focused on the molecular regulation of cell-wall synthesis in Mycobacterium smegmatis. Across projects, I combine bacterial genetics, protein biochemistry, microscopy, and quantitative analysis to test how molecular mechanisms shape cellular behavior.

The through-line

From individual proteins to coordinated cell biology

A bacterial cell is not a collection of independent pathways. It must coordinate envelope construction, growth, division, and responses to stress while keeping its physical barriers intact. In my doctoral research, I studied how regulatory proteins and metabolic intermediates help connect these processes in mycobacteria.

That work shaped the questions I bring to research: What is the mechanism? Where and when does it operate in the cell? What evidence would distinguish a direct effect from an indirect one? And how far can we generalize from a molecular result to a physiological outcome?

My research experience has moved between genetics, biochemical mechanism, protein interaction, and cell-level imaging. Each approach provides a different kind of evidence; the strongest explanations emerge when those lines of evidence are tested against one another.

Research program · Doctoral work

Coordinating the mycobacterial cell envelope

My PhD research asked how bacteria coordinate two major layers of the cell envelope even though those layers are assembled through distinct biosynthetic pathways.

01

PgfA: linking peptidoglycan and mycolate synthesis

The question. How does a growing mycobacterial cell coordinate its peptidoglycan (PG) layer with its lipid-rich outer membrane, or mycomembrane?

The approach. I combined targeted mutagenesis and complementation with chemical perturbations, fluorescent metabolic probes, and quantitative fluorescence microscopy. HADA/NADA labeling helped assess PG synthesis, while DMN-trehalose provided a readout related to mycolic-acid biosynthesis.

What the work showed. My first-author study identified a regulatory role for the polar growth factor PgfA in coordinating polar PG synthesis with mycolate synthesis. The results support a model in which availability of the shared metabolic intermediate trehalose monomycolate (TMM) helps connect envelope-building pathways. I also characterized regulatory threonine phosphorylation sites in PgfA’s intrinsically disordered region and investigated their relationship to cell-wall organization and antibiotic susceptibility.

Why it matters. The work provides a mechanistic example of how a bacterium can coordinate distinct envelope layers rather than regulate each pathway in isolation.

First-author paper · Journal of Bacteriology (2026)

02

MurJ: understanding the regulation of an essential flippase

The question. How is MurJ, the essential lipid II flippase that moves a peptidoglycan precursor across the membrane, regulated in mycobacteria?

The approach. I used CRISPR interference, domain-deletion genetics, and complementary experiments to examine MurJ’s regulatory architecture and test a proposed interaction between MurJ and the regulatory protein CwlM. The interaction model was evaluated using AlphaFold2 Multimer, a mycobacterial two-hybrid assay, and co-immunoprecipitation.

What the work showed. The work supported opposing regulatory roles for MurJ domains: the periplasmic substrate-binding domain promotes activity, while the cytoplasmic domain can inhibit it. It also showed that the regulatory architecture is not functionally interchangeable between M. smegmatis and M. tuberculosis. Across three complementary approaches, we did not validate the previously proposed MurJ–CwlM interaction.

Why it matters. This project reinforced the importance of testing attractive mechanistic models with independent evidence—and of distinguishing a result in a model organism from a conclusion about a related pathogen.

Doctoral research · 2021–2024

03

CwlM and MurA: regulation at the start of peptidoglycan synthesis

The question. How does the phosphorylation state of CwlM influence MurA, the enzyme that catalyzes the first committed step in peptidoglycan precursor synthesis?

The approach. In this collaborative project, I contributed to experimental design, molecular biology, protein purification, in-vitro enzyme assays, and characterization of a proposed protein–protein interface.

What the work showed. The study found that phosphorylated CwlM stimulates MurA through a charged interface away from CwlM’s phosphorylation site, providing insight into how a regulatory signal can influence enzyme activity through protein-level interactions.

Why it matters. This work complements my studies of envelope coordination by examining regulation at an earlier step in the pathway: the production of the building blocks required to construct the cell wall.

Collaborative publication · Frontiers in Microbiology (2025)

04

SepIVA: balancing elongation and septation

The question. How do regulatory modifications help mycobacteria balance cell elongation with septation during division?

My contribution. As a co-author, I contributed experimental data and analysis and reviewed the manuscript for a study examining arginine methylation sites on SepIVA.

Why it matters. Cell-wall synthesis is not only a matter of making the right materials; cells must also organize where growth happens and when division proceeds. This project connects envelope biogenesis to the spatial and temporal control of cell shape and division.

How I work

Building an explanation from multiple kinds of evidence

My training has emphasized connecting molecular hypotheses to measurable phenotypes and being explicit about what each experiment can—and cannot—establish.

Genetics and perturbation

Allelic exchange, targeted and site-directed mutagenesis, CRISPRi, complementation, domain deletions, and phenotypic characterization to ask whether a component is necessary and how a change affects the system.

Biochemistry and enzymology

Recombinant protein expression and purification, in-vitro enzyme activity assays, and work with peptidoglycan synthases and hydrolases, kinases, phosphatases, and proteases to probe mechanism.

Protein interactions

Co-immunoprecipitation, mycobacterial protein-interaction assays, BioLayer Interferometry, and structural prediction—using complementary methods rather than treating one assay as definitive.

Microscopy and metabolic labeling

Live-cell confocal and epifluorescence microscopy, time-lapse imaging, fluorescent cell-wall probes, and metabolic labeling to examine where cellular processes occur.

Quantitative image analysis

Automated image segmentation and MicrobeJ, alongside R/RStudio and GraphPad Prism, to turn microscopy and experimental measurements into interpretable quantitative comparisons.

Physiology and antimicrobial response

Antimicrobial susceptibility measurements, including MIC and disk-diffusion approaches, to connect changes in cell biology with measurable outcomes while avoiding overinterpretation of any single phenotype.

Academic growth

Questions I learned to ask—and questions I want to ask next

My doctoral work began with the machinery of bacterial cell-wall construction: the proteins, domains, and biochemical steps required to build and maintain an envelope. Over time, the questions expanded from the function of individual components to the coordination of pathways, the spatial organization of synthesis, and the physiological consequences of perturbing those systems.

That progression has made me especially interested in the interface between mechanism and context. How do bacteria sense chemical or physical features of their environment and translate those signals into coordinated physiological changes? Why might genetically similar cells behave differently? When does that variation matter for stress responses or antimicrobial outcomes?

These are developing interests, rather than claims about a completed research program. They provide a bridge from my established background in bacterial physiology and cell biology toward broader questions about microbial adaptation.

Developing directions

From monoculture toward host–microbe systems

My doctoral work focused on bacteria in laboratory culture. I am interested in extending a mechanistic approach to questions about how microbes respond to more complex environments, including host-associated settings. Host–microbe symbiosis, bacterial engineering, and genetic or phenotypic screening are areas I am interested in exploring as my research develops.

For me, the appeal is not simply to move into a new system; it is to preserve the ability to ask tractable mechanistic questions while accounting for the environmental and biological context in which microbes live.

Research in practice

Rigor is a process, not a slogan

I value experimental design that makes competing explanations distinguishable, controls that match the claim being made, and independent approaches when a result carries substantial interpretive weight. I try to separate observation from inference, communicate uncertainty honestly, and treat unexpected or contradictory results as information to investigate rather than inconveniences to hide.

I also value collaborative science. My projects have involved different kinds of contributions—from experimental design and hands-on work to analysis, figures, and manuscript development—and have reinforced the importance of making contributions and interpretation clear.
