Philosophy & Values

Be adventurous in the questions we ask and rigorous in the answers we claim. I want to build a research environment where intellectual honesty, curiosity, fairness, and care for people strengthen the science.

Discovery and intellectual honesty

I am drawn to scientific questions that open entirely new directions of discovery. Ambition and novelty are important, but they must be matched by careful methods, systematic troubleshooting, and evidence strong enough to support the conclusions we make. Unexpected, noisy, and negative results are part of science—not embarrassments to hide. They are reasons to check controls, understand limitations, and ask better questions.

Rigor without losing perspective

In a publish-or-perish system, high-impact, intellectually rigorous work can create real opportunities. I would encourage trainees to give ambitious publication targets a thoughtful chance when the work warrants it, while being honest about the costs in time, money, and uncertainty. The goal is not to chase prestige indefinitely or spend resources polishing a result beyond what is useful. We should validate a promising direction, assess the evidence and novelty, and make a deliberate submission plan with the trainee's goals and timeline in view.

A paper's impact factor is not the only measure of its value. I want trainees to publish work they are proud of, whether it appears in a high-profile journal or a more specialized one, and to understand the trade-offs behind publication decisions.

Independence

The goal of mentorship is not to create people who agree with the PI. It is to help trainees become experts in their niches: able to read critically, develop their own interpretations, defend a position with evidence, recognize uncertainty, and decide what to do next. Disagreement is welcome when it is thoughtful and grounded. We should examine evidence together and, when possible, identify experiments or analyses that distinguish competing explanations.

The PI may need to make time-sensitive decisions—for example, when a grant deadline is imminent—but that should not erase the trainee's expertise or close the door to revisiting a question. A strong argument from a trainee should be able to change the lab's direction.

Collaboration over competition

Scientific progress is collective. People should not have to compete with labmates for basic respect, credit, support, or belonging. We should share methods and reasoning, give constructive feedback, acknowledge contributions, and discuss project roles and likely authorship early. A disappointing result is not a disappointing person, and rigorous critique should never require humiliation.

Integrity

Fabricating data is incompatible with the lab's values. Honest mistakes and imperfect experiments call for examination, learning, and better safeguards. Repeated carelessness, failure to communicate, or intentional misconduct can affect colleagues and the integrity of the work, so they require clear and proportionate intervention. Formal research-integrity concerns must be handled through applicable institutional procedures, with fairness and due process.

Inclusion, respect, and belonging

Respect means taking colleagues’ time, identities, boundaries, and different working styles seriously. Inclusion is more than saying everyone is welcome; it requires attention to how opportunities, recognition, resources, introductions, and chances to lead are actually distributed. I want to be a collaborator, advocate, and mentor who takes trainees’ goals seriously and helps them build the relationships, skills, and visibility that serve their chosen futures.

People should be able to ask questions, admit mistakes, raise concerns, and disagree without humiliation. That does not mean avoiding accountability: harmful conduct must be addressed clearly and fairly, with attention to the facts, the people affected, and appropriate institutional processes.

Mentorship should serve the trainee’s future

Each person’s career belongs to them. I want trainees to feel respected, supported through difficulty, proud of their work, and equipped to pursue the futures they choose—even when those futures differ from mine. The practical expectations and approach to training are set out in Mentoring & Training.

What success looks like (to me)

Success includes rigorous, meaningful science and publications that create opportunities. It also means trainees become capable and independent scientists, develop networks beyond the PI, feel that their work and ideas matter, and have a fair chance to pursue the futures they want. I hope the lab is known for exciting questions, honest evidence, generous collaboration, and a culture where people are treated as whole human beings—not simply as sources of data.

My commitments

  • Communicate early about obstacles, delays, and unexpected results; increase check-ins when a pattern suggests a trainee needs more structure.
  • Use mistakes as opportunities to understand causes and prevent recurrence, while treating deliberate fabrication and harmful repeated negligence as serious matters.
  • Discuss authorship, project roles, and credit early and revisit them as work evolves.
  • Make opportunities and sponsorship responsive to trainees’ goals, with attention to fairness, access, and belonging.
  • Be willing to listen, change my mind, and revise these principles when evidence or experience shows a better way.
