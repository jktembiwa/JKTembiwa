Lab wiki · Mentorship & teaching

Help people become independent scientists who can thrive.

Our approach to training, feedback, career support, and the shared responsibility of building a rigorous and humane research environment.

Our goal: independent scientists who can thrive

Mentorship is not about producing trainees who can execute protocols or depend on a PI to make decisions. It is about helping people become rigorous, creative, independent scientists who understand their fields, take ownership of their work, and have the confidence and skills to shape their own futures.

Every trainee brings different strengths, experiences, learning needs, and career aspirations. The goal is not to mentor everyone identically, but to give everyone the guidance, opportunities, and support they need to grow.

Rigor is non-negotiable; learning is a process

We value careful experimental design, appropriate controls, reproducibility, documentation, and evidence-based interpretation. Unexpected results and negative data are part of science, not failures to be hidden.

Trainees should feel comfortable bringing forward confusing, messy, or disappointing results. We will troubleshoot systematically, check controls and methods, and investigate what the evidence can actually support. Changes to established protocols should have a rationale and be documented so that others can understand what was done, why, and whether the result can be reproduced.

Our guiding principle: Be adventurous in the questions we ask and rigorous in the answers we claim.

Independence grows through practice

Trainees should learn to explain their reasoning, evaluate competing interpretations, identify weaknesses in their experiments, and propose ways to distinguish between hypotheses. The PI's role is to guide inquiry, not to own every conclusion. When a trainee disagrees with the PI, we should examine the evidence together and identify what would resolve the disagreement.

Training will be adapted to the individual. Some trainees may initially need close demonstrations and structured practice; others may be ready for greater autonomy sooner. The goal is to build competence and sound judgment—not to keep people dependent on supervision.

Clear communication and accountability

Expectations should be discussed early, before misunderstandings become recurring problems. Trainees should know how to raise concerns, communicate delays, ask for help, and report unexpected results.

Individual development plans and regular check-ins can help us revisit scientific progress, mentoring needs, professional development, and career goals. Their purpose is to make support and expectations clearer, not to create unnecessary administrative work.

When communication or performance falls short, the first step is to understand what is happening and clarify expectations. Repeated problems may require a more structured plan, with a clear purpose, agreed expectations, and a date to reassess whether additional support is still needed. Accountability should be proportionate: an honest experimental mistake is an opportunity to learn; repeated negligence that affects others requires intervention; deliberate fabrication is incompatible with membership in a research lab.

A collaborative lab, not a competitive one

Students should not have to compete with labmates for the PI's attention, recognition, or approval. We will encourage collaboration, shared problem-solving, and respect for one another's contributions.

Authorship and project responsibilities should be discussed early and revisited as work evolves. Decisions should reflect actual intellectual and practical contributions to the research and manuscript, informed by relevant journal and institutional guidance. When projects overlap, we should look for fair arrangements that preserve meaningful contributions and, where scientifically justified, allow related work to develop into distinct papers.

Mentorship should serve the trainee's goals

Not every trainee wants the same career. Some will pursue academic research; others may prefer industry, teaching, government, science communication, or paths that do not yet exist.

We should discuss career goals regularly and help trainees build the experience, skills, relationships, and accomplishments relevant to their chosen directions. This includes meaningful sponsorship: recommending trainees for appropriate opportunities, connecting them with colleagues, and advocating for their work. A trainee's future belongs to them. We should support their development even when their goals differ from our own or eventually take them beyond the lab.

People come before productivity

Trainees are whole people, not data-generating machines. Personal difficulties, illness, uncertainty, and changes in priorities are part of life, and people should not have to disclose private details to be treated with respect.

When a trainee's capacity changes, we should work together on realistic priorities, timelines, workload adjustments, and handoffs where needed. The PI also has a responsibility to help protect collaborators from avoidable disruption rather than leaving one trainee to carry the entire burden. Support and accountability can coexist: we can respect privacy, adjust expectations where reasonable, and maintain communication about work without equating temporary difficulty with a lack of scientific potential.

A respectful and inclusive community

Scientific talent does not excuse behavior that undermines colleagues or makes people afraid to participate. We expect people to treat one another with respect, give credit fairly, and contribute to an environment where trainees can ask questions, disagree, and learn without humiliation.

Concerns about harmful behavior should be taken seriously and assessed fairly. People should have an opportunity to respond and demonstrate change, while the lab takes reasonable steps to protect those affected. Scientific excellence and good community membership are distinct responsibilities; we will not treat one as a substitute for the other.

What success looks like

Success is not measured only by publication count, journal impact, or how much work a trainee completes while in the lab. We want trainees to leave with strong scientific judgment, technical competence, intellectual ownership, and the ability to pursue their own ideas. We want them to be proud of their work, equipped to navigate their careers, and able to look back on their time in the lab knowing they were respected—even when they struggled, made mistakes, or disagreed with the PI.

Ultimately, the goal is to help people become scientists who can thrive independently, contribute generously to their communities, and remain excited about discovery.
